# Ground 7: The Tide Supplier, supply-chain maps (run 2026-11-19/21)

Method in one line: I named the shifts I could back with filing numbers, mapped buyers, suppliers, bottleneck and who keeps the margin, then placed universe names using RPO/backlog (XBRL `RevenueRemainingPerformanceObligation`, 10-Q text, or the EX-99.1 earnings release on EDGAR when the 10-Q has no number), latest-quarter revenue and operating-margin change (XBRL quarterly facts), and crowding from `triage/metrics.csv` (ret_3y, P/E and EV/EBIT vs own 10-year median, val_disc_avg; split-adjusted). Evidence cache: `data/g7_evidence/*.json` (one file per company, 165 names) and `data/g7_evidence/table.csv`. Crowding rule used in the CSV: high = 3y return >= 300%, or >= 150% with val_disc_avg >= 0.5, or val_disc_avg >= 1.0; med = 3y return >= 75% or val_disc_avg >= 0.25; low = everything else. Where margins sit 2x or more above their own 10-year median I say so, because then "near its historical multiple" hides how much is already priced: at median margins the multiple would be far higher. Context that matters for every section: AI-infrastructure stocks have been sold hard since June 2026 (Nasdaq-100 lost roughly $2.5T on 5 Nov 2026 per press reports I used only as a lead: https://yourstory.com/ai-story/ai-stocks-crash-wall-street-trillion-rout, https://stockanalysis.com/news/ai-data-center-suppliers-lose-4-trillion/), so many 3-year returns below still look large while prices sit 30-58% under their 52-week highs (universe.csv, 2026-11-18 close).

## 1. AI data-center buildout

Evidence the shift is real (EDGAR XBRL capex, `PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment` / Amazon `PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets`): Amazon $175.7B in Jan-Sep 2026 vs $92.3B a year earlier (10-Q filed 2026-10-30, acc 0001018724-26-000040); Alphabet $132.0B vs $63.6B (10-Q 2026-10-29, acc 0001652044-26-000084); Meta $83.1B vs $48.3B (10-Q 2026-10-22, acc 0001628280-26-066773); Microsoft $106.1B for Jan-Sep 2026 vs $53.2B (quarters from 10-Qs 2026-04-29, 2026-10-28 and 10-K 2026-07-29). The four together: $497B vs $257B, +93%. Oracle spent $28.5B in Jun-Aug 2026 vs $8.5B (10-Q 2026-09-11). NVIDIA's quarter to 2026-07-26: revenue $96.2B, +106%, operating margin 66.2% (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm).

Chain map. Buyers: Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle, neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius) and the model labs behind them. Layer 1, compute and memory: NVDA, AVGO, AMD, MU, SK hynix, TSM. Layer 2, network and optics: ANET, CIEN, LITE, COHR, CRDO, ALAB, MRVL, APH, GLW, FN. Layer 3, systems assembly: DELL, SMCI, CLS, FLEX, JBL, SANM. Layer 4, storage: STX, WDC. Layer 5, power and cooling inside the hall: VRT, ETN, NVT, TT, MOD, AAON (mapped in section 2). Layer 6, shells and landlords: EQIX, DLR. Layer 7, building it: EME, FIX, DY, J (section 3). Bottlenecks in 2026: leading-edge wafers and HBM memory, then grid power and switchgear, then electricians.

Who captures the margin. The chip and memory layer and the hard-disk duopoly keep it: latest-quarter operating margin NVDA 66.2% (vs 60.8% a year earlier), AVGO 53.9% (vs 36.9%), MU 80.4% (vs 23.3%, quarter to 2026-05-28), STX 48.8% (vs 26.4%), WDC 46.7% (vs 28.1%). The assemblers carry the volume and keep little: DELL 11.5%, SMCI 10.4%, CLS 7.9% (down from 10.2% while revenue rose 83%), FN 9.9%, JBL 5.1%, FLEX 4.9%. That is the "supplier with no pricing power" trap in plain numbers.

Crowded (the obvious, priced names). Optics and networking: LITE +1,143% in 3 years, CLS +765%, CRDO +576%, CIEN +493%, COHR +485%, ANET +260%. Components: APH +249% at P/E 44 vs 15.8 own median; GLW +304% at 58 vs 23. Storage: WDC +709%, STX +611% (STX at 37x vs 12x). Systems: DELL +346% at 33.5x vs 12.3x (RPO $132B, +200%). Semicap: LRCX +202% at 36x vs 18x, AMAT +136% at 40x vs 16x, KLAC +166% at 39x vs 21x. Power-in-the-hall: VRT +338% (organic orders +100% in Q3 2026 per its EX-99.1). All of these have real backlog; none is priced for anything less.

Cheap-looking traps. MU at 9.4x vs 12.9x is cheap only because operating margin is 80% against a 28% ten-year median: peak-cycle memory. WDC 11.5x vs 19.9x on a 47% margin vs 10% median: same story. SMCI at 7.2x vs 18.4x with RPO $5.67B (+451%) is an assembler with a 7% full-year margin and a 2024 auditor resignation in its history: no pricing power. NVDA at 30.4x vs 50.4x is the headline winner itself, not a tide supplier, owned by everyone (55 analysts). ORCL at 20.9x vs 29.4x with RPO $664B (+46%, 10-Q 2026-09-11) is a buyer of NVIDIA capacity reselling it with heavy capex ($55.7B in FY ended May 2026) and customer concentration; the margin flows up to the chip layer, and the stock is 54% off its 52-week high for that reason.

Placed from this chain (second-order, less obvious): EQIX (shells and interconnection; RPO $15.9B, +25% y/y at 2026-09-30; operating margin 20% vs 17.6% median; P/E 76 vs 95.8 own median; 3y +41%; low crowding; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1101239/000110123926000180/eqix-20260930.htm). Every other AI-chain name I place sits in sections 2 and 3 because its demand also comes from utilities, factories or telecom, which is what makes it a tide supplier rather than an AI bet. Verdict on the compute/network/optics/memory layer itself: fully priced; no name qualifies.

## 2. Electricity demand and the grid rebuild

Evidence the shift is real. Utility capex (XBRL, FY2022 to FY2025): Southern $7.9B to $12.7B, Duke $11.4B to $14.0B, Entergy $5.1B to $7.7B, PPL $2.2B to $4.0B, Exelon $7.1B to $8.5B; Q1 2026 vs Q1 2025: Duke +30%, Entergy +36%, PPL +33%, Southern +21%. AEP's 10-Q (filed 2026-10-28) describes a $100.4B five-year capital plan and says new data-processing load is already lifting commercial and industrial sales (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/4904/000000490426000072/aep-20260930.htm). On the equipment side: GE Vernova RPO $196.7B at 2026-09-30, +45% y/y, Q3 operating margin 11.0% vs 3.7%, with "favorable pricing" at Gas Power and Power Transmission (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1996810/000199681026000163/gev-20260930.htm); Eaton Electrical Americas backlog $15.99B vs $12.01B (+33%), organic orders +39%, book-to-bill 1.3, total backlog $26.2B (10-Q 2026-11-03); Caterpillar RPO $49.4B vs $22.5B; Powell backlog $2.7B vs $1.4B; Trane backlog $12.5B, +73%.

Chain map. Buyers: regulated utilities (SO, DUK, AEP, ETR, D, PPL, NEE), independent power producers (CEG, VST, NRG, TLN) and hyperscalers building their own generation behind the meter. Suppliers: generation equipment (GEV gas turbines, CAT and CMI gensets and recip engines, BKR aero-derivative turbines, BE fuel cells), transmission and distribution equipment (ETN, HUBB, POWL switchgear, GEV/Prolec transformers, VMI poles), cooling for the load (TT, VRT, NVT, MOD, AAON), installers (PWR, MTZ, section 3), copper (FCX, SCCO). Bottleneck: large gas turbines (multi-year slot queues), transformers and medium-voltage switchgear, and interconnection approvals.

Who captures the margin. The equipment makers with sold-out factories: GEV's operating margin went from 3.7% to 11.0% in a year; Powell's full-year margin is 21% against a 3% ten-year median; Hubbell 20.7% vs 14.1%; Eaton about 19% (FY2025 pre-tax plus interest over revenue) vs 15.8%. Utilities capture volume, not margin: their returns are set by regulators, and AEP's own filing spends a paragraph on affordability, which is where that model breaks. Wind is the losing sub-shift: GE Vernova wind turbine orders were 438 units in Jan-Sep 2026 vs 560 a year earlier.

Crowded. GEV +491% in 3 years (EV/EBIT 137x); VST +271% at 45x vs 13.6x own median; TLN +562%; BE +1,230%; CEG +119% at 39.8x; PWR +228% at 87x vs 30x; MTZ +218%; VRT +338%. The copper leg is priced too: SCCO at 28.8x vs 19.8x, FCX at 33.5x vs 21.8x (earnings held down by the Grasberg disruption), and copper miners are price takers, so I did not place them.

Overlooked or still near history (placed): HUBB (organic Electrical Solutions growth above 20% led by data centers, Utility Solutions growth in distribution, transmission and substations, P/E 25.4 vs 22.5, 3y +47%); ETN (P/E 32.2 vs 26.6, 3y +55%, the biggest backlog in the group); TT (backlog +73%, P/E 30.7 vs 24.4); CMI (Power Systems record on data-center gensets while trucks are weak, P/E 19.8 vs 13.9); CAT (RPO doubled, P/E 28.8 vs 16.1, more priced); NVT (organic +44%, but 25x vs 17.6x); POWL (backlog +93% and the best pricing evidence in the group, but +456% in 3 years, so flagged high).

## 3. Building it: specialty contractors, engineers, heavy civil (data centers, fabs, grid, roads)

Evidence the shift is real (RPO or backlog, latest quarter vs a year earlier): Quanta $41.75B vs $20.97B (+99%); Comfort Systems $17.81B vs $9.38B (+90%); MasTec $19.1B vs $11.7B (+63%); EMCOR $18.57B vs $12.61B (+47%); Dycom $12.24B vs $7.99B (+53%; organic Communications $10.98B, +37.5%); IES $2.8B vs $1.3B (+116%); Sterling combined backlog $6.14B vs $3.31B at Dec 2025; Jacobs $28.9B, +27% (TTM book-to-bill 1.4x); Fluor new awards $19.7B in Jan-Sep 2026 vs $10.8B. Comfort Systems' 10-Q says same-store revenue rose 46% "especially strong in the technology sector" (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1035983/000110465926118159/fix-20260930x10q.htm).

Chain map. Buyers: hyperscalers and colocation developers, chip and battery fabs, utilities (section 2), state DOTs, telecom carriers. Suppliers: mechanical and electrical contractors (EME, FIX, IESC), utility and pipeline contractors (PWR, MTZ), fiber and telecom contractors (DY), site and civil (STRL, GVA, TPC), designers and program managers (J, ACM, TTEK, FLR), equipment rental (URI, SUNB, HRI), aggregates and cement (VMC, MLM, CRH, AMRZ). Bottleneck: skilled electricians and pipefitters, not steel or concrete.

Who captures the margin. Labor scarcity has turned contractors into price setters, at least for now: EMCOR's operating margin 10.1% in 2025 vs a 5.1% ten-year median; Comfort Systems 16.6% in Q3 2026; IES 14.4% in its June quarter vs a 4% median; Sterling 19.6% in Q3; Granite 6.4% in 2025 vs 2.2%. These are project margins: EMCOR earned 2.9% in 2020, so the multiples below look tamer than they are. Materials do not show the tide at all: Vulcan revenue -5% in Q3 2026, CRH +3% with RPO -9%; aggregates pricing power is real, but volumes are not rising with this shift, so I placed none.

Crowded. FIX +493% at 39x vs 20.5x; STRL +330% at 29x vs 12.4x; IESC +392% at 23x vs 8.2x; TPC +1,091% at 63x vs 9x; PWR and MTZ (section 2); URI +137% at 27x vs 15.8x.

Overlooked or near history (placed). EME: RPO +47%, revenue +29% in Q3, P/E 22.2 vs 19.4, diversified across data centers, manufacturing, healthcare and water. DY: backlog +53%, P/E 24.2 vs 24.8, 58% below its 52-week high after August guidance came in under estimates and organic Communications EBITDA margin slipped to 13.6% from 14.9%; part of the growth is the acquired National Technology Integrators, and the customer list is concentrated in carriers plus hyperscaler long-haul fiber. J: backlog +27%, EV/EBIT 24.3 vs 24.7 own median (its GAAP P/E of 64 is distorted), but adjusted net revenue grew only 8% while gross revenue rose 34%. GVA: record $7.5B of committed and awarded projects, P/E 28 vs 35, but it depends on federal highway money whose current authorization (IIJA) ran through fiscal 2026, so the next bill matters more than data centers.

## 4. Defense rearmament

Evidence the shift is real (RPO or total backlog, latest vs a year earlier unless stated): Lockheed Martin $292.0B vs $179.1B (+63%); RTX $340B vs $251B (+35%); General Dynamics $220.3B on 2026-10-04 vs $136.5B at the end of the prior quarter, with a defense book-to-bill of 8.5x in Q3 on Virginia- and Columbia-class submarine awards (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/40533/000004053326000038/gd-20261004.htm); Huntington Ingalls $86.2B vs $53.1B at Dec 2025, after $43.1B of awards in nine months including nine Block VI Virginia-class boats; Northrop Grumman $109.6B (+20%); L3Harris $42.0B (+19%); BWX Technologies $8.56B (+16%); Kratos $2.65B (+79%); Elbit $28.1B at Dec 2025 (+24%) for the European and Israeli side.

Chain map. Buyer: the US Department of Defense plus allied governments. Primes: LMT, RTX, NOC, GD, HII, LHX. Tier two: propulsion, actuation and parts (TDG, HEI, CW, MOG-A, WWD, DRS), naval nuclear reactors (BWXT, sole source), materials (CRS, ATI, HWM, HXL), services and IT (LDOS, CACI, BAH, SAIC, PSN). Bottlenecks: shipyard labor, solid rocket motors, castings and forgings, specialty alloys, munitions capacity.

Who captures the margin. Not the primes. Operating margins against own ten-year medians: LMT 10.3% (2025) vs 12.9%; NOC 10.8% vs 11.4% with fixed-price losses on B-21 (a $226M charge in Q1 2025) and Sentinel; GD 10.2% vs 10.9%; HII 5.3% vs 7.5%, now recovering (Q3 2026 7.0% vs 5.0%). The government sets their margin through fixed-price and cost-plus rules. The parts and materials makers keep it: TransDigm 45.6% vs 42.9%; Carpenter 22.5% (fiscal year to June 2026) vs 7.8%, with its alloys segment at a 37.7% adjusted margin "from pricing actions" (EX-99.1 2026-10-22); Howmet 25% vs 13%; ATI 14% vs 8%.

Crowded. ESLT +252% at 61x vs 26x; HWM +333% at 58x vs 37x; ATI +306% at 61x vs 19x; MOG-A +152% at 45x vs 20x; KTOS at 259x; RKLB +1,131%; GE Aerospace +248%; WWD +148% at 43x vs 28x; CW +117% at 34x vs 23x.

Cheap but not a tide supplier. LDOS at 10.6x vs 19.3x looks like the prize until you read the backlog table: total $49.6B vs $47.7B (+4%), Defense +40% to $14.65B but Health -21% to $6.32B (10-Q 2026-11-03). BAH revenue -4% and backlog -2%; PSN revenue +2%; TXT RPO flat. These are government IT and services shops, not rearmament suppliers.

Placed: TDG (the margin captor, at its own P/E history after only +38%), HII and GD (the shipbuilding bottleneck, with backlogs that jumped this year), NOC (low crowding, flat margins), RTX (missiles plus engine aftermarket, more priced), LHX (rocket motors), BWXT (sole-source naval reactors, but margins below history), LMT (biggest backlog jump, weakest margins), CRS (best pricing evidence, flagged high for its +304% run).

## 5. Energy security: natural gas for power and export, plus nuclear

Evidence the shift is real. Baker Hughes Q3 2026 orders $10.76B vs $8.21B (+31%), RPO $46.4B with a record $39.4B in its gas-technology segment (IET), including a 1.1 GW integrated power order for a US technology company; it names data centers, LNG and gas infrastructure as the demand (EX-99.1 2026-10-27). Kinder Morgan's project backlog is $14.4B, of which a record $13.2B is natural gas, with $5.4B added in Q3 (EX-99.1 2026-10-21). Cheniere signed long-term SPAs with QatarEnergy for up to 4 mtpa from the early 2030s, took FID on the first phase of the Sabine Pass expansion, is building Corpus Christi midscale trains 8-9 (~5 mtpa) and raised 2026 EBITDA guidance to $8.75-9.25B from $7.90-8.40B (EX-99.1 and 10-Q 2026-10-29). GE Vernova cites favorable gas-turbine pricing (section 2). On nuclear, Constellation's RPO rose to $11.86B at mid-2026 from $0.85B on long-term power contracts.

Chain map. Buyers: European and Asian gas importers replacing pipeline supply, US utilities and data-center developers adding gas-fired generation. Suppliers: gas producers (EQT, EXE, AR), pipelines (KMI, WMB, DTM, AM, OKE), liquefaction (LNG), equipment (BKR turbines and compressors, GEV, FLS pumps, TS pipe), nuclear (CCJ fuel, BWXT components, CEG and VST plants). Bottleneck: turbines and compressors, pipeline permits, liquefaction trains.

Who captures the margin. Contracted infrastructure and equipment, not the molecule: Cheniere's operating margin 45.6% in 2025 vs a 25% median on fixed liquefaction fees; Kinder Morgan steady at 28% vs 27%; Baker Hughes from a 4% median to about 10-11%. The producers are price takers: EQT revenue -10% in Q3 2026 with RPO -9%; Expand Energy's quarterly margin 19.5% vs 24.4%. I placed no producer.

Crowded. WMB +111% at 32x vs 20x; DTM +132% at 27x vs 12.7x; TRGP +256%; CCJ at 66x; SEI +674%; plus the power producers in section 2. FLS has a record $3.5B backlog (+20%) but guided organic sales down 1.5-2.5% on Middle East disruption, so I left it out.

Overlooked (placed). BKR: three shifts in one order book (LNG, data-center power, pipelines), P/E 18, +49% in 3 years, 31% below its 52-week high; GAAP net income fell to $411M from $609M on $214M of adjustments while adjusted EBITDA rose 14%, which I treat as noise but flag. LNG: 8.9x earnings vs a 12.3x median (EV/EBIT 8.9 vs 14.7) with long fixed-fee contracts; the market is pricing the 2026-2028 global LNG supply wave, which will squeeze the marketing margin (Q3 operating margin 31% vs 41%) but not the contracted fees. KMI: record gas backlog at 22.9x vs 17.4x, +112% in 3 years, so more priced.

## 6. Shifts I tested and dropped

Water: supplier revenue in the latest quarter ran XYL -3%, PNR -4%, CNM +2%, AWK +3%, WMS +5%, VLTO +7%, ZWS +9% (only WTS +19%); XYL's RPO jump to $3.19B looks like a definition change, not demand. No tide in the numbers. Aging population and healthcare capacity: the obvious supplier (WST, GLP-1 injection components) has a 2% three-year revenue CAGR and trades at 57x vs 45x; I could not find a capacity-constrained supplier with backlog disclosure. Software "plumbing": the hint file's ZS, QLYS, GDDY, PLTR, OKTA sell to IT budgets and seats, not to a physical shift, so they fail the definition. Construction materials: pricing power without volume (section 3).

## Why this list looks like this

The list is light on AI and heavy on suppliers that AI shares with other buyers, because the filings show that the AI chain's margin is already taken and already priced. Hyperscaler capex ran $497B in Jan-Sep 2026, +93%. The companies keeping that money (NVDA at a 66% operating margin, MU at 80%, STX at 49%, WDC at 47%) and the optics and networking names that rode it (LITE +1,143%, CLS +765%, CRDO +576%, CIEN +493% in three years) left nothing near its own history except names that are cheap for a reason: MU at 9.4x on peak memory margins, SMCI at 7.2x as a 7%-margin assembler, ORCL at 20.9x as a leveraged reseller of NVIDIA capacity. So every name except EQIX sits in the second ring, where demand comes from at least two buyers. These are utilities running $100B-plus five-year plans (AEP $100.4B) alongside data centers (HUBB, ETN, TT, CMI, CAT, NVT, POWL); contractors whose RPO rose 47-99% across data centers, fabs and the grid (EME, DY, J, GVA); a defense book that jumped (LMT +63%, HII +55%, GD +$84B in one quarter, RTX +35%); and gas equipment and pipes fed by LNG and power at once (BKR orders +31%, KMI's record $13.2B gas backlog, Cheniere's new Qatar SPAs). The margin test splits the list in two. The primes and the contracted pipes grow on volume with flat or falling margins (LMT 10.3% vs a 12.9% median, NOC 10.8% vs 11.4%, GD 10.2% vs 10.9%, BWXT 12.6% vs 15.9%). The real price-setters (TDG 45.6% vs 42.9%, HUBB 20.7% vs 14.1%, EME 10.1% vs 5.1%, CRS 22.5% vs 7.8%, POWL 21% vs 2.9%) are either fully priced or flattered by margins two to seven times their own history, which makes "near its historical P/E" weaker comfort than it looks: at median margins EME would trade near 44x and CRS near 70x. Only five names earn a low crowding flag (BKR, TDG, HII, NOC, EQIX). Two are flagged high and kept only because their pricing evidence is the best in their chains (CRS, POWL). I stopped at 24 because copper (FCX 33.5x vs 21.8x, SCCO 28.8x vs 19.8x), the power producers (VST 45x vs 13.6x, TLN +562%) and the whole compute, network, optics and memory layer are priced, and because water, healthcare capacity and software plumbing showed no tide in the filings. The selloff since June 2026 has already cut many of these names 30-58% from their highs (DY -58%, CRS -56%, BWXT -50%, VRT -48%). That helps entry prices, but it is also a warning that backlog can be repriced if the AI financing chain (OpenAI, Oracle, the neoclouds) wobbles, which is why the top of the list is made of companies whose orders also come from utilities, navies and LNG buyers. The best Ground 7 bets in November 2026 are suppliers fed by two or more shifts whose multiple still sits near its own history, led by Baker Hughes, Hubbell and TransDigm; the pure AI suppliers are the obvious, priced names and should be left alone.
