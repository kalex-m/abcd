# Why the Ground 1-4 lists look like this (Phase 2, 2026-11-19)

Data: EDGAR XBRL frames (latest fiscal year, usually FY2025; FY2026 for Jun-Sep year ends), yfinance prices at the 2026-11-18 close. Scores are averages of percentile ranks; see `scripts/p2_grounds.py` for every weight. Diversity cap: at most 4 names per yfinance industry and 4 China/Hong Kong names per list. Full ranked lists are in `ground_N_full.csv`.

## Ground 1: The Bruised Essential (325 eligible -> 25)

Eligibility was price at least 30% below its 3-year high or a multiple at least 35% under its own 10-year median, plus positive free cash flow, revenue no more than 35% below its 4-year peak and a gross margin within 15% of its 5-year median. The 25 that came out on top are down a median 61% from their 3-year highs (the eligible pool: 40%), have lost 36% over three years while the eligible pool gained 22%, and trade at a median 16x earnings against a median 42x for their own last decade, yet their gross margins are where they were (median gm_hold 1.02) and they kept shrinking their share counts (median -3.9% over 5 years). The list is dominated by one event, not by many separate bruises: application software and IT services (ADBE 12x vs a 46x history, FICO 17x vs 50x, INTU 18x vs 51x, NICE 8x vs 41x, Gartner 15x vs 42x, EPAM, FIS, Fiserv) have been repriced as if AI agents will hollow out seat-based and services revenue, and the rest is consumer brands past their peak (LULU 8x, DECK), China internet (BABA, NTES, FUTU, EDU) and a few physical-economy names (APTV, CTVA, CHTR, LII, POWL). That means Ground 1 as screened is mostly a test of one question, whether the software repricing is a cycle or a substitution, and Phase 3 has to treat it with the Zoom standard; the genuinely "critical, battered, model intact" industrial names (building products, rails, chemicals) mostly did not score because their multiples never fell far below their own history. Takeaway: the cheapest high-quality bruises in this market are in software, and that is exactly where "down for a reason" is most likely, so the default for every name on this list is suspicion.

## Ground 2: The Mid-Rebuild (516 eligible -> 25)

Eligibility was at least one rebuild signal (restructuring charges above 1% of revenue over 3 years, divestiture proceeds above 2% of market cap, impairments above 2% of revenue, three or more 8-K Item 5.02 officer/director filings, or a completed Item 2.01 transaction) plus a stock that lagged (3-year return under 30% or 20% off its high) and positive operating cash flow. The top 25 are down a median 45% from their 3-year highs, have filed a median nine Item 5.02 8-Ks in three years (eligible pool: seven), carry a median 1.3% of revenue in restructuring charges, and their operating cash flow has outgrown net income since 2023 (positive ocf_growth_vs_ni for all 25), which is the exact "GAAP looks worse than cash" pattern the ground is built to find. The list is heavy in packaged food (GIS, CPB, CAG, LW, plus KHC just outside) where volumes are falling and the GLP-1 question hangs over the category, and it includes classic portfolio reshapers (HON splitting into three, APTV separating its EDS business, BF-B, MGM, ADM, AGCO, CE paying down the debt from its 2022 purchase of DuPont's Mobility & Materials unit). A rebuild signal in XBRL is only a clue: the count of Item 5.02 filings mixes CEO changes with routine director turnover, and impairments can mean the old business is dying rather than being rebuilt, so Phase 3 has to check whether each plan has delivered its earlier targets. Takeaway: the screen found plenty of companies that are changing inside while their stocks sag, but packaged food rebuilds have the strongest smell of slow decline wearing a new slogan.

## Ground 3: The Toll Road (97 eligible -> 25)

Eligibility was a 10-year median operating margin of at least 22%, capital spending under 10% of revenue (or under 22% for margins above 33%, which lets rails and pipelines in), a stable margin through 2019-2025 or no more than three revenue down-years, a 5-year average return on capital of at least 8% and at most one negative free-cash-flow year; banks and insurers are excluded. The winners have a median 10-year operating margin of 39% and a median 38% return on capital, which is the toll-road shape, and the scoring then rewards those whose multiple has slipped below their own history: the median top-25 name trades at 21x earnings against a 32x own-history median, while the eligible pool trades above its history (median val_disc_avg +19%). That is why the list mixes the obvious, still-fully-priced tolls (Visa 33x, Mastercard 37x vs 36x, SPGI 29x vs 30x, MSCI 38x vs 37x, TransDigm 31x vs 31x) with tolls whose price has slipped (Copart 20x vs 32x, FactSet 21x vs 30x, MarketAxess 24x vs 42x, Paychex 23x vs 28x), plus names that are not tolls at all and will be thrown out in Phase 3 (FUTU is a leveraged retail brokerage, NetEase is a game studio, ADBE and INTU are software subscriptions rather than flow fees). Union Pacific and the other rails screen in as tolls but rank outside the top 25 because they trade above their own history (UNP 25x vs a 19x median). Takeaway: the true toll roads are almost all priced like toll roads; the only ones on sale are those where the market fears the flow itself is shrinking or being re-routed (dealer-to-client bond trading, financial data seats, salvage auctions, payroll), and each of those fears needs its own test.

## Ground 4: The Quiet Vault (60 eligible -> 25)

Eligibility was net cash or very low net debt (net cash at least -5% of market cap or net debt under ~0.75x operating profit), a free-cash-flow yield on enterprise value of at least 5%, no more than one negative free-cash-flow year in ten, and no net dilution over five years; banks, insurers and REITs are excluded. Only 60 of 1,254 companies pass all four, which is itself a finding: a real war chest plus a 5%+ cash yield is rare in this market. The top 25 yield a median 9.9% free cash flow on enterprise value (8.4% after stock pay), hold net cash of about 2% of market cap, and have cut their share counts by a median 9.8% in five years, while their stocks are up only 8% over three years against 38% for the eligible pool. The list splits into three kinds: software and IT services that turned into cash machines the market now distrusts (ADBE, NICE, ACN, CTSH, EPAM, INTU, CHKP, Genpact), cyclicals at or near peak earnings where a high yield is normal and not a mispricing (EOG, CF, NVR, PHM, SF), and a few genuinely dull compounders (Cirrus Logic, Dolby, Logitech, Acuity, Ameriprise, Cardinal Health). Attention is not low: the median top-25 name has 20 covering analysts, more than the eligible pool's 14, so "nobody cares" is not literally true here; the edge, if any, is that people care and still refuse to pay. Two numbers need auditing in Phase 4 before any of these counts as a vault: Ameriprise's "net cash" sits inside a regulated financial group, and Cardinal Health's cash is offset by large opioid settlement payments. Takeaway: the vault list is short and noisy, and the most interesting members are the boring hardware and licensing names, not the famous software ones.

## Cross-check vs yfinance (25 names per ground)

`triage/xcheck_ground_N.csv`. After adding capitalized software to capex (the first pass missed it for FICO, NICE, FIS, MKTX, which overstated their free cash flow by up to a few percent of revenue), EDGAR and yfinance agree to within 5% on revenue, net income, operating cash flow and capex for 82 of 100 rows. The rest are explained: FUTU and NetEase file in HKD/CNY with a USD convenience translation in XBRL, so yfinance shows local currency (EDGAR used); Altria and Molson Coors report revenue including excise taxes in the `Revenues` tag (yfinance shows net revenue; affects margins slightly, not cash flow); Caesars and CF differ on net income attributable vs including non-controlling interests; Mastercard, Dolby, Pilgrim's capex differs by other capitalized items. EDGAR values kept in every case.
