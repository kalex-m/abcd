## 1. The one-paragraph truth

Ameriprise is a 10,000-advisor US financial-planning firm with two product arms attached. The first is Columbia Threadneedle, an active asset manager with $743B of AUM. The second is RiverSource, a life and annuity insurer. It makes money four ways. (1) Fees on $729B of wrap (fee-based advisory) assets and $1.23T of total client assets, about 57% of which is paid straight back out to advisors as "distribution expense" (Q3 2026: $1,911M on $3,371M of AWM net revenue). (2) Spread income on $27.0B of client cash sweep and the $24.7B Ameriprise Bank, which is rate-driven. AWM net investment income was $461M in Q3 2026 against $74M of deposit interest. (3) Asset-management fees at about 48bp, at a 45.7% margin. (4) Annuity and life spread and fees of about $200M of pretax a quarter. Adjusted operating earnings were $4.16B over the twelve months to 2026-09-30 (Q3-26 10-Q ROE reconciliation), with Advice & Wealth Management (AWM) making 65% of segment pretax profit in 2025, up from 52% in 2019 (inline XBRL segments, 10-Ks FY19-FY25). Subsidiaries sent $4.06B of net dividends up to the parent in 2025 (FY25 10-K, MD&A liquidity table), and the parent sends almost all of it to shareholders: $3.94B over the last four quarters, of which $3.3B went to buybacks. It is a planning-led distribution business that captures a market-linked fee and a rate-linked spread on its clients' savings, then shrinks its share count about 7% a year.

## 2. Inside the company

**Leadership.** Jim Cracchiolo (age 67) has been chairman and CEO since the 2005 spin-off from American Express. Walter Berman has been CFO for the same era. No named successor appears in the 2026 proxy beyond boilerplate on "strong succession planning", so key-person and succession risk is real and unaddressed.

**Pay design (2026 proxy).** The annual cash bonus is 70% financial and 30% "business and strategic". The 70% splits into net revenues 15%, earnings 25%, EPS 20%, ROE ex-AOCI 20% and balance sheet 20%. For 2025 the pool funded at 155% of target. Cracchiolo's total direct pay was $31.0M: $1.25M salary, $7.75M cash bonus and $22.0M long-term award. Half of the long-term award is PSUs (performance share units) on 3-year average ROE and EPS CAGR with a TSR modifier. The 2023-2025 PSUs paid 145% of target on 51.9% average ROE and 17.2% EPS CAGR, against targets of 41.9% and 14.1%. CEO pay ratio is 232:1. Ownership guideline is 10x salary. The design rewards per-share earnings and ROE, which buybacks mechanically help. ROE ex-AOCI also ignores the bond-loss hit that just cut GAAP equity by $1.4B. A 2025 say-on-pay vote of 89% shows no shareholder revolt.

**What management emphasises now vs three years ago.** The FY22-era message was de-risking the insurer: a "shift to lower-risk products, including the exit from variable annuities" with living benefits (still in the FY25 10-K forward-looking list). By 2026 the message is:
- the "integrated technology platform" and AI. On the Q3-26 call, three quarters of advisor practices were said to use AI-generated insights.
- the bank: pledged-asset lending up more than 50% year on year.
- the financial-institutions channel, with 258 Huntington advisors and about $32B of assets arriving through mid-2027.
- a defensive line on recruiting.

Both the Q3-26 call's first question (Jefferies) and its last (Evercore, "the elephant in the room") were about press reports of a broad advisor-retention package. Berman: "we are not making changes." Cracchiolo: "there's no program," followed by a long riff about anonymous sources. Berman added that competitors' deals have "seven, eight-year paybacks on cash" and that he "scratch[es] my head".

**What changed in the recruiting numbers.** Experienced-advisor recruits ran 82, 73, 79 (Q1-Q3 2025), 91 (Q4 2025), then 61 and 79 (Q1 and Q3 2026). The Q3-26 call language went from "irrational competition" (prior calls, per the Evercore question) to "we have selectively adjusted" offers.

**Capital allocation.** It is unchanged and explicit: return about 90% of operating earnings. That was 93% in Q3 2026, over 100% in Q4 2025, and a new $5.5B authorisation was approved 2026-09-29 to run through 2028-09-30 (Q3-26 10-Q). Holding company targets are about $2.0B of excess capital and $2.3B of available liquidity (Q3-26 call).

Takeaway: a long-tenured, buyback-first management team running a very consistent playbook, now forced onto the defensive on advisor retention for the first time in years, with no visible succession plan.

**Reading the numbers.** TTM adjusted operating EPS is $44.42, so the stock is at 11.3x trailing and 10.4x the Q3 ex-unlocking run rate ($48.28). AWM is the engine:
- TTM net revenue per advisor reached a record $1.24M, up 13%.
- The pretax margin held at 29.3%.
- Management says "core distribution earnings", meaning non-spread, rose more than 20% and are now the majority of segment profit (Q3-26 ER p.3). That matters because it reduces dependence on cash-sweep spread, which was the 2022-2023 earnings tailwind.

The spread piece is still large. Q3 2026 AWM net investment income of $461M less $74M of deposit interest is about $387M a quarter of pre-cost spread, against $989M of segment pretax. Halving it would cut AWM pretax by roughly a fifth.

Asset Management is shrinking in assets but not in profit. It has had net outflows in six of the last eight quarters, about $50B cumulatively. Yet pretax rose 18% to $308M at a 45.7% margin, on markets and Seligman performance fees, with a stable ~48bp fee rate. RPS is flat at about $200M a quarter as the living-benefit variable annuity book runs off ($0.8B of related fund outflows in Q3).

**Cash conversion.** For a financial, owner cash is what the subsidiaries can dividend up. That was $2.78B in 2023, $3.94B in 2024 and $4.06B in 2025, split AWM $2.49B, AM $0.93B, RPS $0.6B a year (FY25 10-K). Parent operating cash flow was $3.87B in 2025, against adjusted operating earnings of about $4.0B. The consolidated "$8.3B OCF / 18.7% FCF yield" on the screen is bank and annuity balance-sheet flow and should be ignored. Capex is about $0.16B a year and SBC $0.21B. Adjusted ROE ex-AOCI was 56.2% TTM on $7.54B of equity ex-AOCI (Q3-26 ER p.27). That number is high because the business needs little equity, not because of leverage tricks: parent debt is only $3.5B.

Takeaway: the earnings are real and distributable, about 95% converted to parent cash, and the mix has moved toward the fee-based advice business. But roughly a fifth of AWM profit is still rate spread, and AM is a melting-asset, rising-margin business that markets are currently flattering.

## 5. Ground(s) and evidence

**G4 Quiet Vault: pass on quality, fail on "quiet mispricing".** The evidence for the quality side:
- 11 analysts and 4.3% short interest (yfinance 2026-11-20), with no 13D.
- Share count down from 168M (2016) to 87.0M (2026-09-30).
- ROE above 50% for three years.
- 2025 TSR of -7% vs +15% for S&P 500 Financials (2026 proxy p.51), and the stock is -16% from its 52-week high of $572.56 at $480.29.

On mispricing: 11.3x trailing adjusted earnings is roughly the 10-year median P/E of 12.5 (card). The "vault" is a 9.0% capital-return yield ($3.94B TTM over a $43.5B market cap), not a hidden asset.

**G5 Many-Weathers.** Year-on-year growth in segment pretax earnings, 2020-2025:

| | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AWM | -12% | +32% | +26% | +30% | +13% | +6% |
| AM | +5% | +57% | -23% | -15% | +28% | +10% |
| RPS | -34% | +29% | +40% | -21% | +6% | +17% |
| Total (ex-Corporate) | -14% | +38% | +13% | +9% | +15% | +8% |

| Growth correlation | Value |
|---|---|
| AWM vs AM | 0.07 |
| AWM vs RPS | 0.54 |
| AM vs RPS | 0.19 |

(Six observations, so this is indicative only.) The low AWM/AM correlation is real but has one cause: rates. In 2022-2023 rising rates lifted AWM spread income (+26%, +30%) while hurting bond-heavy AM (-23%, -15%). In 2020, a falling-rate plus crash year, everything except AM fell and total pretax dropped 14%. These are two weathers, rates and equity markets, not three independent engines. AWM is now 65% of the profit pool, so diversification is shrinking each year. Verdict on G5: weak pass. The segments offset each other in a rates shock, but not in an equity bear market, which is the scenario that matters most for a fee business.

Takeaway: AMP qualifies as a well-run, under-loved compounder, but neither ground identifies a hidden value. The case rests on arithmetic, not on something others are missing.

## 6. What the market is pricing

Inputs to `scripts/valuation.py`:
- Owner cash $4.0B: TTM adjusted operating earnings ex-unlocking were about $4.24B, minus about 5% retained to grow subsidiary capital. This matches the $3.94B actually returned plus the $0.28B rise in equity ex-AOCI over four quarters.
- Net cash 0. I treat excess capital as offsetting part of the parent debt and leave the rest inside the business. Counting the $2.0B of excess capital would add $23 a share.
- 87.0M shares, price $501.42, discount rate 10% (a market-levered financial), terminal growth 3%.

Result: the price implies owner cash **falling 2.3% a year** for five years before fading to 3%. That becomes 0.0% at an 11% discount rate and -7.6% at 8%. Flat owner cash forever at 10% is worth $460 a share.

Over the last five years total segment pretax grew every year except 2020, and per-share earnings compounded about 24% a year (card, EDGAR). On a no-growth bar the implied path looks too pessimistic. There are three honest reasons the market will not pay more:
1. Earnings are at a cyclical high on two counts: equity markets near records drive fees, and short rates are rising again, which drives spread.
2. AM is losing about $6-9B a quarter.
3. The advisor-recruiting war could raise the 57% payout to advisors.

A 20% earnings reset in a bear market, like 2020's -14% pretax, with -3% growth thereafter, gives $386 (scenario below). The market is pricing roughly a midpoint between "flat forever" and "bear reset", which I read as modestly too pessimistic for a business still growing wrap assets 12% and returning 9% a year.
