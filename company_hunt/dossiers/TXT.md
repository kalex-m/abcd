## 1. The one-paragraph truth

Textron builds small and mid-size business jets and turboprops (Cessna Citation, Beechcraft King Air) and services the roughly global fleet of them, builds commercial and military helicopters and the Army's new MV-75 Cheyenne tiltrotor at Bell, and makes armoured vehicles, drones, landing craft and contracted adversary-air training at Textron Systems; a small captive finance arm lends to aircraft buyers. It makes money in three different ways: Aviation earns an 11-12% segment margin on a jet cycle that is currently strong (backlog $8.0B at 2026-10-03, 51 jets delivered in Q3-26 vs 42), plus an aftermarket that grew 10% in Q3 and is "over 30% of New Textron revenue" (Q1-26 call); Bell earns a thinning 7-9% margin while it spends to bring MV-75 into production; Systems earns 13-14% on cost-plus and fixed-price defense work. In 2025 the three A&D segments plus Finance made $1.22B of segment profit on $11.6B of revenue (FY25 10-K, XBRL), and the company turned $14.8B of total revenue into $0.93B of free cash flow. Two items make reported earnings look better than the cash business: about $270-280M a year of non-service pension income from a $2.6B-overfunded pension plan ($210M in 9M-26, release), which is real accounting income but not cash, and an "adjusted EPS" that adds back the LIFO inventory provision ($116M in 9M-26), a genuine inflation cost. Strip both and the $6.60 adjusted EPS guide shrinks to roughly $4.60 of core operating earnings per share. Takeaway: a decent, cyclical aerospace manufacturer whose headline 12.5x P/E overstates how cheap it is.

## 2. Inside the company

Leadership changed hands this year. Scott Donnelly, CEO since 2009, handed over to Lisa Atherton (previously head of Bell and before that Textron Systems) in January 2026 and stays as Executive Chairman; his last call was 2026-01-28. CFO David Rosenberg replaced Frank Connor on 2025-03-01 (DEF 14A 2026). Textron Aviation got a new CEO, Brian Rohloff, a 29-year Textron Aviation veteran (Q3-26 call). Pay design (DEF 14A 2026): the annual bonus was 60% enterprise net operating profit and 35% manufacturing cash flow in 2025 (paid at 128.6% of target), with ESG swapped for a 5% "AI adoption and utilization" component from 2026; long-term pay is 50% PSUs earned on three-year average ROIC (50%), cumulative manufacturing cash flow (30%) and relative TSR vs the S&P 500 (20%). The 2023-25 PSU cycle paid 93.3%: ROIC came in at 11.6% against a 12.4% target, cash flow $2,716M against $2,683M target. That is the right design for a capital-heavy manufacturer, with one caveat: the committee can adjust ROIC and cash flow for "items not contemplated", and it excluded the powersports disposal in 2025. Strategy under Atherton is visibly different from Donnelly's last three years. Donnelly's language on the 2026-01-28 call was "ongoing investments in new products and programs, drive organic growth and margin expansion"; within three months Atherton had folded the money-losing eAviation segment into Aviation and Systems (effective 2026-01-04), announced the Industrial separation (2026-04-30), sold it (2026-11-18), pulled MV-75 capex forward by about two years (2026 capex guide $650M, up $383M, later cut by $100M), and told the Q3 call she is "open to something transformational" on M&A. The 2026-11-18 8-K says the $1.1B goes to "additional share repurchases in 2027". Capital allocation has been steady: buybacks of $0.8-1.2B a year since 2021, a token $0.08 dividend, no acquisitions in five years, and 9M-26 repurchases of $616M for 7.0M shares (about $88 each). Takeaway: the new CEO is acting faster and more portfolio-minded than her predecessor; the open question is whether "transformational" means a large acquisition that would undo the clean buyback story.

## 6. What the market is pricing

Reverse DCF (scripts/valuation.py, 2026-11-21): owner cash $0.80B, which is 2025 FCF $0.93B less SBC $0.08B less about $0.09B for the sold Industrial segment (my estimate: $145M segment profit less tax and its capex); net cash -$1.4B (section 4, conservative, already including the $1.1B proceeds); 169.4M shares (yfinance); price $82.79; EV $15.4B; 9% discount rate; 2.5% terminal growth. The price implies owner cash growing 6.1% a year for five years, fading to 2.5% by year 10 (3.1% at an 8% discount rate, 8.8% at 10%). Zero-growth value is $44. Is 6% plausible? New Textron revenue grew 4.5% a year from 2019 to 2025 (A&D trio $9.77B to $11.48B) and 8% in 9M-26; backlog is $19.0B (+6%); MV-75 has a requested $2.3B FY2027 budget line (Q3-26 call) and will move into production later in the decade; buybacks shrink the share count 4-6% a year, but that is per-share growth, not owner cash growth. Against that, 2026 cash is depressed by MV-75 capex pulled forward, while the LRIP phase is "largely fixed price" and already carries a $110-150M loss-of-margin charge (Q2-26 and Q3-26 10-Q). Takeaway: the market prices a reasonable mid-single-digit path; it is neither too pessimistic nor too optimistic.

## 8. The thesis in one sentence, and why the market is not seeing it

The best case is that New Textron, a pure aerospace and defense company with $19B of backlog, an overfunded pension and $1.1B of sale proceeds plus about $0.8B a year of free cash going into buybacks (enough to retire roughly 20% of the share count over two to three years under the new 35M-share authorization), is trading at a conglomerate multiple. But I cannot find what the market is missing: the headline 12.5x P/E is flattered by LIFO add-backs and pension income, core earnings put it at about 18x, and the separation has been public since 2026-04-30. Takeaway: there is no edge here beyond "a reasonable company at a fair price".

## 9. The industry picture

Business aviation is in a strong but supply-constrained phase: Textron's Q3-26 orders were its best third quarter since 2023, aftermarket revenue grew 10% on high fleet use, and the bottleneck is parts (engines, extrusions, spars, APUs), not demand (Q3-26 call). Parts availability is "back to where we were in about the 2021 timeframe", not 2019. Jet demand historically falls hard in recessions: Aviation revenue fell 23% and profit 96% in 2020 (FY21 10-K). In defense, the proposed $1.5 trillion FY2027 US defense budget (cited on the Q1-26 call) favours munitions, uncrewed systems and rapid fielding; Textron's exposure is MV-75 (the Army's largest new aviation program, accelerated by about 2.5-3 years per the Q4-25 call), Systems land vehicles (65 MSFVs for Ukraine through Foreign Military Sales, XM30 prototypes with American Rheinmetall, the Marine Corps ARV) and ATAC adversary-air training. Winners in this industry are the companies with an installed base and sole-source production positions; Textron has both in Citation/King Air service and, once production starts, MV-75. The loser risk is fixed-price development and early-production contracts, which have hurt several large primes; MV-75 LRIP is largely fixed price. Takeaway: Textron sits in the middle of the pack: good franchises, less pricing power than engine or components makers, and one big fixed-price production bet.

## 14. What would make this thesis wrong

Sell or stay away if any of these happen: (1) an MV-75 charge above $150M at LRIP award, or any second unfavourable catch-up on the program, which would show the fixed-price production phase is losing money; (2) Bell full-year 2027 segment margin below 7% (9M-26: 7.2%); (3) Textron Aviation backlog below $7.0B (2026-10-03: $8.0B) or two straight quarters of jet deliveries down year over year, which would mark the top of the jet cycle; (4) the Industrial proceeds go to an acquisition above $2B instead of buybacks ("open to something transformational", Q3-26 call); (5) 2027 manufacturing cash flow before pension below $700M with capex back toward $400M; (6) manufacturing net debt to capital above 30% (now 19%). On the upside, I would revisit if the price fell below $65 (bottom of my range) with the jet backlog intact.
