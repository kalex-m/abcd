## 1. The one-paragraph truth

Smurfit Westrock buys wood and waste paper, turns them into containerboard and paperboard in its own mills, and then (the part that matters) converts most of that paper into corrugated boxes, folding cartons, bag-in-box and displays in hundreds of plants sited near consumer-goods, food and e-commerce customers in 40 countries. It is the July 2024 merger of Smurfit Kappa (Irish, Europe and Latin America, the accounting acquirer) and WestRock (U.S.). 2025 net sales were $31.18B: North America 58%, EMEA and APAC 35%, Latin America 7%; corrugated and paper 77%, consumer packaging 23% (MTP deck slide 8). It makes money on the spread between box and paper prices and its costs for fiber, energy, freight and labor; that spread is set by the industry operating rate, so earnings move with the cycle, not with any pricing power the company holds alone. Segment adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, after management's add-backs) was $5.10B in 2025, but depreciation of about $2.75B and capex of $2.19B eat most of it, so GAAP operating income was $1.72B and free cash flow $1.20B on a $45B balance sheet (FY25 10-K; card). It is a giant, essential, integrated converter of a commodity whose returns on capital (ROIC 4.1% in 2025) have been below its cost of capital since the merger.

## 5. Ground(s) and evidence

G1 Bruised Essential: an essential product, a temporary bruise, and a price that treats the bruise as permanent. Essential: corrugated boxes carry most of the world's packaged consumer goods; no customer above 10% of sales and no substitute at scale (FY25 10-K). The bruise: about $700M of unplanned 2026 freight and energy cost (Q3-26 release), European gas at roughly $30/MMBtu, ten times U.S. levels (Q3-26 call), weak Mexican and U.S. converting volumes, and 2026 guidance cut twice in three months, from $5.0-5.3B (2026-02-11) to $5.0-5.3B reaffirmed (2026-04-30), $4.9-5.1B (2026-07-29) and about $4.7B (2026-10-28). The 2025 guide followed the same path ($5.0-5.2B in May 2025 to $4.9-5.1B in October, actual $4.94B). Evidence that the bruise is temporary: containerboard sold out in North America; announced paper price increases of net $170/ton in North America and net €140/ton in Europe, with full cost recovery of the latest round promised for Q1 2027; sequential pricing up 5% in North America and 2% in Europe from Q2 to Q3 2026; October volumes flat year on year (Q3-26 call). Evidence against: the 2026 bruise is the second year of guidance misses, EBITDA margin fell from 16.0% (9M 2025) to 14.3% (9M 2026), and the medium-term plan assumes no North American paper price increases at all through 2030 (MTP deck slide 5, footnote 1), so the new increases are upside to the plan only if they stick, while the plan's volume assumption (1.6% North American market growth a year) has not been met in any year since the merger. Takeaway: the essential part is beyond dispute; whether the bruise is temporary depends on a price increase announced into weak volumes and an antitrust probe of exactly that pricing behaviour.

## 8. The thesis in one sentence, and why the market is not seeing it

Thesis: if the $170/ton North American and €140/ton European paper increases stick and EBITDA returns to $5.5B+ in 2027-28, SW is worth $46-56 and pays 4.5% while you wait. Why the market may not be seeing it: it is seeing it; 15 analysts cover the stock, the mean target is $53.85 (yfinance 2026-11-20), and the recovery story is the house story. What the market may be underweighting is the other side: the DOJ grand jury subpoena of 2026-09-22 into containerboard pricing, disclosed in the 10-Q on 2026-11-04, a day the stock rose from $42.97 to $44.72 (yfinance), while the recovery thesis rests on exactly the coordinated-looking price increases a criminal probe examines. Takeaway: there is no hidden edge here; the asymmetric fact is a risk the market is shrugging off, not an upside it is missing.

## 9. The industry picture

Containerboard demand tracks real consumer-goods volume, roughly GDP minus a little in developed markets (the plan assumes 1.6% a year in North America, 1.7% in Europe, 2.0% in Latin America, MTP deck slide 5, sourced to Numera), plus e-commerce and the substitution of plastic. Supply is lumpy: big mill conversions and closures swing operating rates, and prices follow operating rates. North America: SW and International Paper are the two largest integrated producers (Fastmarkets snippet on the subpoena), followed by Packaging Corp of America (the margin leader at 11.8x EV/EBITDA) and private Pratt and Georgia-Pacific; SW says it is "sold out in all containerboard grades" (Q3-26 release). Europe: energy is the swing factor; the CEO said on 2026-10-28 that many producers "cannot afford to run the mills" at $30/MMBtu gas, which is the classic setup for closures and then a price recovery, and SW's kraftliner mills that self-generate energy are the low-cost end. Latin America is SW's best business (21-22% margin) and it is consolidating it (CMPC Chile). Winners in this cycle are the integrated, low-cost, energy-self-sufficient producers with converting density; losers are non-integrated European recycled mills on spot gas. SW sits on the winning side in Europe and LATAM; in North America it owns WestRock's assets, which earned 32% less EBITDA in FY2023 than FY2022 and are being rebuilt. Takeaway: the industry is a mature cyclical with rational-looking pricing, and the antitrust question is whether it is too rational.
