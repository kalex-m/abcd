## 1. The one-paragraph truth

Smurfit Westrock buys wood and waste paper, turns them into containerboard and paperboard in about 60 large mills, and then (the part that matters) converts most of that paper into corrugated boxes, folding cartons, bag-in-box and displays in hundreds of plants sited near consumer-goods, food and e-commerce customers in 40 countries. It is the July 2024 merger of Smurfit Kappa (Irish, Europe and Latin America, the accounting acquirer) and WestRock (U.S.). 2025 net sales were $31.18B: North America 58%, EMEA and APAC 35%, Latin America 7%; corrugated and paper 77%, consumer packaging 23% (MTP deck slide 8). It makes money on the spread between box and paper prices and its costs for fiber, energy, freight and labor; that spread is set by the industry operating rate, so earnings move with the cycle, not with any pricing power the company holds alone. Segment adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, after management's add-backs) was $5.10B in 2025, but depreciation of about $2.75B and capex of $2.19B eat most of it, so GAAP operating income was $1.72B and free cash flow $1.20B on a $45B balance sheet (FY25 10-K; card). It is a giant, essential, integrated converter of a commodity whose returns on capital (ROIC 4.1% in 2025) have been below its cost of capital since the merger.

## 2. Inside the company

Leadership: Tony Smurfit (62) is President and Group CEO, the third generation of the founding family at Smurfit Kappa, and owns 1,602,433 shares (about $65M at $40.33; DEF 14A 2026, ownership table as of 2026-02-25); CFO Ken Bowles is also from Smurfit Kappa, and the North America CEO is Laurent Sellier, Europe CEO Saverio Mayer, LATAM CEO Alvaro Henao (MTP deck slide 4). WestRock's management is essentially gone: Smurfit Kappa people run every region. Two directors (Terrell Crews, Lourdes Melgar, both WestRock-side) left at the 2026 AGM, cutting the board to 12 (8-K 2026-02-27). Pay design (DEF 14A 2026): bonus 35% adjusted EBITDA and 35% free cash flow; PSUs 40% relative TSR, 30% cumulative EPS, 30% return on capital employed; that is a sound design that does reward cash and returns. Strategy actually underway: the "owner-operator model" (P&L ownership pushed to each plant), closure of loss-making plants and mills (headcount cut by over 3,000 in 2025, Q4-25 release https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2005951/000110465926012986/tm265780d1_ex99-1.htm; loss-making corrugated plants "fewer than 30", Q3-26 call), "rebalancing the long paper position" in North America (WestRock sold too much paper on the open market; the plan is to convert more in-house), and $4B of growth capex through 2030 in projects averaging under $4M each with none above $200M (MTP deck slides 14, 30). Bolt-on M&A has restarted: CMPC's Chilean containerboard and corrugated business for $420M, closing H1 2027 (Q3-26 10-Q, Note 1). Language shift: in 2024-2025 every release led with "integration" and the $400M synergy target (achieved by end-2025, FY25 10-K risk factors); since February 2026 the language is "Medium-Term Plan", "~$7bn Adjusted EBITDA 2030", "performance-led culture" and "capacity for share buybacks from 2027" (MTP deck slide 5). On the Q3-26 call the CFO called the 2026 shortfall "essentially timing" and said nothing in the plan "from a structural perspective has changed". The antitrust case and the DOJ subpoena were not mentioned in prepared remarks and no analyst asked about them (Q3-26 transcript, full text search). Takeaway: a competent, family-led operator with a sensible pay plan is running WestRock's U.S. assets harder, but it is selling a 2030 target while missing its own one-year guidance.

Sizing the antitrust exposure (my estimate; the company says it cannot estimate a range). Facts: the civil case (Artuso Pastry Foods v. Packaging Corporation of America et al., N.D. Ill. No. 1:25-cv-08856, per WebSearch snippets of Bloomberg Law and Packaging Dive) alleges a Sherman Act conspiracy from 2020-11-01 to the present on containerboard, linerboard sheets and finished boxes; the motion to dismiss was denied 2026-09-04; the DOJ grand jury subpoena followed on 2026-09-22 and reportedly also went to International Paper (Fastmarkets/Packaging Dive snippets). Precedent: the 2010 Kleen Products case in the same court sought about $11B; International Paper, Temple-Inland and Weyerhaeuser settled for $354M in 2017, and WestRock's predecessor and Georgia-Pacific won summary judgment, affirmed by the Seventh Circuit (WebSearch snippets, Justia). My inputs: SW's U.S. corrugated and containerboard sales of roughly $10B a year (North America segment $18.6B less consumer packaging and Canada/Mexico; not disclosed, my estimate) over a six-year class period gives about $60B of affected commerce. Scenarios: (a) civil only, Kleen-like: $0.1-0.3B; (b) DOJ finds something but SW is not the lead defendant: 2-3% of commerce in settlements plus fines, $1-2B; (c) criminal plea or indictment against SW subsidiaries: single damages at a 5% overcharge are $3B, trebled exposure $9B, realistic settlement plus fine $4-6B. With weights 60/30/10 (no indictment, no amnesty disclosure so far), the expected cost is about $1.2B, or $2.20 a share; scenario (c) alone is $8-11 a share. Takeaway: the balance sheet carries $14.4B of net debt and leases against a business valued at $35.6B of EV, so equity holders own a thin, levered slice that a criminal antitrust outcome could cut by a quarter to a half.

## 5. Ground(s) and evidence

G1 Bruised Essential: an essential product, a temporary bruise, and a price that treats the bruise as permanent. Essential: corrugated boxes carry most of the world's packaged consumer goods; no customer above 10% of sales and no substitute at scale (FY25 10-K). The bruise: about $700M of unplanned 2026 freight and energy cost (Q3-26 release), European gas at roughly $30/MMBtu, ten times U.S. levels (Q3-26 call), weak Mexican and U.S. converting volumes, and 2026 guidance cut four times in effect, from $5.0-5.3B (2026-02-11) to $5.0-5.3B reaffirmed (2026-04-30), $4.9-5.1B (2026-07-29) and about $4.7B (2026-10-28). The 2025 guide followed the same path ($5.0-5.2B in May 2025 to $4.9-5.1B in October, actual $4.94B). Evidence that the bruise is temporary: containerboard sold out in North America; announced paper price increases of net $170/ton in North America and net €140/ton in Europe, with full cost recovery of the latest round promised for Q1 2027; sequential pricing up 5% in North America and 2% in Europe from Q2 to Q3 2026; October volumes flat year on year (Q3-26 call). Evidence against: the 2026 bruise is the second year of guidance misses, EBITDA margin fell from 16.0% (9M 2025) to 14.3% (9M 2026), and the medium-term plan assumes no North American paper price increases at all through 2030 (MTP deck slide 5, footnote 1), so the new increases are upside to the plan only if they stick, while the plan's volume assumption (1.6% North American market growth a year) has not been met in any year since the merger. Takeaway: the essential part is beyond dispute; whether the bruise is temporary depends on a price increase announced into weak volumes and an antitrust probe of exactly that pricing behaviour.
