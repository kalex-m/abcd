## 1. The one-paragraph truth

Mobility Global sells data about cars. Two-thirds of the money (CARFAX, $1,142M of 2025's $1,750M revenue, Form 10 MD&A) comes from vehicle history reports and the dealer tools built on them: dealers pay subscriptions so they can put a free CARFAX report on every used-car listing, plus listing, service-loyalty and "Homegrown" products; lenders, insurers and consumers pay per report. The report is valuable because CARFAX collects records from more than 100,000 service shops, DMVs and other suppliers (40 billion records at Q3 2026, release 2026-11-12), and because shoppers have learned to ask for it. The other third (B2B, $608M in 2025) is the old IHS Markit/Polk automotive unit: new-car registration data, forecasts for automakers and suppliers, automotiveMastermind (predicts which dealer customers will buy) and Market Scan (lease and loan pricing). The business is 81% subscription (Q3 2026: $389M of $478M), needs almost no capital (capex $31M on $1.4B of nine-month revenue), and earns a 41% adjusted EBITDA margin. GAAP profit looks small ($42M in Q3 2026) because $74M a quarter of amortization comes from S&P Global's 2022 purchase of IHS Markit and because the company now carries $2.0B of notes that paid S&P Global a dividend on the way out. Takeaway: a CARFAX toll on US used-car transactions with a lower-quality B2B data business attached, now levered at about 2.2x.

## 2. Inside the company

Leadership is the S&P Global Mobility team carried over. CEO William (Bill) Eager ran CARFAX and was promoted to President, S&P Global Mobility on 2025-08-15 (Form 10, executive compensation section); CFO Matthew Calderone; Scott Fredericks runs CARFAX; Joseph LaFeir runs the B2B unit ("Mobility Business Solutions"). The board chair is Joseph Hinrichs (former CSX CEO), and the board was seated on 2026-07-01 with Mark Peek as Audit chair and Monique Leroux as chair of Nominating and Compensation (8-K 2026-07-02, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2090312/000110465926080006/tm2619098d1_8k.htm). Pay design is the weak spot. Under S&P Global, the executives' long-term pay vested on (a) S&P Global cumulative adjusted EPS and (b) cumulative "CARFAX U.S. Adjusted EBITA" over three years; the 2025-2027 CARFAX target is an absolute EBITA figure, and the 2025 annual bonus pool was funded 100% on CARFAX U.S. EBITA (target $467.1M, actual $479.3M) (Form 10, pp. 135-137). Those are absolute profit targets, not per-share or return-on-capital targets. The first MBGL-designed grants seen so far are time-vested: one-time RSUs of $1.25M for Fredericks and $1.0M for LaFeir, approved 2026-10-21, vesting over three years with no performance condition (8-K 2026-10-23, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2090312/000209031226000029/mbgl-20261023.htm). The real design will appear in the first proxy before the 2027-05-18 annual meeting (10-Q Item 5). Strategy as stated on the Q3 call has three planks: AI applied to proprietary data (report processing cut "from 2 business days to less than 2 hours"), growth inside the largest dealer groups, and "One Mobility Global" cross-selling (first combined Mastermind + CarCare product launching 2027). The operating change underway is mechanical: moving hundreds of systems off S&P Global's transition services within 18 months of the spin, at about $100M of one-time cost through 2027, half of it in 2026 (CFO, Q3 call). Capital allocation so far: $0.06 quarterly dividend (0.24/yr, about 1% yield) and an $800M buyback authorization with $350-450M targeted by end-2027 (release 2026-11-12). There is no three-year history of shareholder letters to compare, but the language did shift within 2026: the August call still defended the late-2025 switch to selling dealers a bundled "lifetime package"; in November the CEO said that switch "was not delivering" by June 2026 and sales went back to a-la-carte products. Takeaway: a competent operating team whose pay has always rewarded absolute EBITA, and whose one real misstep in the record (the bundle) was reversed within about six months.

The core of the economics is CARFAX: revenue compounded 11% a year from 2023 to 2025 on price and dealer new-business growth (S&P Global 10-K 2025 MD&A: "price increases and new business growth within the Dealer business"), with segment adjusted EBITDA margin rising from 46% to 47% and reaching 49% in Q3 2026 even after standalone costs were allocated. But growth is slowing: 12% in 2024, 10% in 2025, 8% in 9M 2026 (CARFAX U.S. +8% in Q3, its "third consecutive quarter of accelerating" growth after the bundle stumble, per the CFO). B2B is the weaker half: revenue grew only 3% in 2024 and 6% in 2025, its adjusted EBITDA fell from $203M to $197M in 2025, and its Q3 2026 margin was 27%, down 550bp, once standalone costs were allocated (Q3 call). Management guided B2B growth in Q4 to be "moderated" relative to Q3 and said margins in B2B will be "a focus for next year" (Q3 Q&A). Unit economics are those of a data toll: operating expenses beyond data acquisition and sales are small, depreciation is $15M a year against capex of $16M (9M 2025) to $31M (9M 2026, inflated by system build-out), so maintenance capex is about $15-25M, or about 1% of revenue. Working capital is negative (unearned revenue $87M, accrued and other current liabilities $162M vs receivables $226M at 2026-09-30, 10-Q). Return on capital depends entirely on the denominator: on tangible capital (PP&E $25M, working capital about zero) it is effectively unlimited; on total invested capital including $8,845M goodwill and $3,566M intangibles from the IHS Markit deal (equity $9,827M + debt $1,981M - cash $351M = $11.5B), after-tax adjusted operating profit of about $0.55B is a 4.8% return. That 4.8% belongs to S&P Global's purchase price, not to a buyer at today's $8.7B EV, where the same profit is 6.3%.

Cash conversion needs a standalone adjustment, and this is where the kill test was too generous. Carve-out FCF of $460M (2025) was struck with almost no interest ($13M) and with taxes paid inside S&P Global. Standalone, three things change: (1) interest on $2.0B of notes is about $111M a year (5.05% x $650M + 5.45% x $650M + 6.05% x $700M); the first cash interest payment of about $60M falls in Q4 2026; (2) the CFO said cash taxes will run $80-90M a year above the GAAP tax provision "for the next 12 years" because the acquired-intangible amortization is mostly not tax-deductible (Q3 call), and the GAAP tax rate guide rose to 32% from 28-31%; (3) standalone costs cut adjusted EBITDA margin by about 150bp in 2027 versus the 2025 carve-out baseline of 41% (CFO, Q3 call). Q3 2026 FCF of $186M "does not include any interest payment or payment for taxes accrued" (CFO), so it overstates the run rate. My normalized 2027 owner cash flow: revenue about $2.0B (7% growth on the $1,877M 2026 guide midpoint), adjusted EBITDA about $790M (39.5%), less SBC about $30M, capex about $45M, cash interest about $100M net of interest income, cash taxes about $190M (GAAP provision of about $110M plus $85M) = about $425-440M of FCF after SBC, or about $1.47 per share, before roughly $50M of 2027 one-time stand-up costs. That is a 6.2% yield at $23.93. Takeaway: the franchise is real and the CARFAX half is excellent, but standalone cash flow per share in 2027 will be roughly flat with the 2025 carve-out figure because interest and the tax drag absorb the growth.

## 5. Ground(s) and evidence

**G3 Toll Road (CARFAX).** The evidence for a toll is in the numbers and in the competitive structure. CARFAX revenue grew every year in the record (928 to 1,142, 2023-2025) through a used-car market that was flat to soft, with a 46-49% segment margin, 81% subscription mix, and growth that the S&P Global 10-Ks attribute to "price increases and new business growth within the Dealer business" (FY2023 and FY2025 10-K MD&A). The asset is three-sided: 100,000+ service shops feeding records one shop at a time (built over decades), 60 million consumers registered for CarCare, and dealers who need the report on their listings because shoppers expect it. Named competitors are Experian (AutoCheck), J.D. Power and Cox Automotive (Form 10, competition section); none has displaced CARFAX's consumer brand. The toll is not absolute: suppliers have consolidated and raised fees, some data agreements "allow them to cancel on short notice" (Form 10, risk factors, p. 43), and the go-to-market stumble in H1 2026 shows dealers do push back on packaging. The B2B segment is not a toll: 3-6% growth, 27-35% margins, and exposure to automaker planning budgets.

**G2 Mid-Rebuild.** The rebuild is a separation, not a turnaround: (1) systems migration off S&P Global within 18 months of 2026-07-01; (2) about $100M of one-time stand-up spend through 2027; (3) standalone costs already in the Q3 base (B2B margin down 550bp, CARFAX at 49% after allocation); (4) a reversed dealer go-to-market change in June 2026 now showing three quarters of acceleration at CARFAX U.S.; (5) a stated 2027 focus on B2B margins. The rebuild signal that would matter, B2B margin recovery toward 32-35%, has not shown up yet.

**Rough SOTP (my assumptions).** CARFAX 2026 segment adjusted EBITDA about $595M (9M $446M plus a Q4 at the 9M rate) at 13x (Equifax 12.8x, Copart 13.1x; no premium for the brand) = $7.7B; B2B about $195M at 9x (below TransUnion's 11.9x for a slower, lower-margin business) = $1.8B; corporate costs are now allocated to segments (Q3 corporate adjusted EBITDA only -$4M), so I take no separate deduction. EV $9.5B less net debt $1.72B = $7.8B, or $26.4 per share. At 15x / 10x it is $30.5. Takeaway: the toll is real in CARFAX, the rebuild is real but administrative, and neither ground produces a deep discount at $23.93.

## 6. What the market is pricing

Reverse DCF with `scripts/valuation.py` (2026-11-21): starting unlevered owner cash $0.51B (my 2027 FCF after SBC of about $0.435B plus after-tax interest of about $75M), net debt $1.70B, 294.84M shares, price $23.93, 10-year horizon, 2.5% terminal growth. At a 9% discount rate the price implies unlevered cash growth of 4.0% a year for five years, fading to 2.5%; at 8% it implies 1.1%; at 10% it implies 6.6%. The zero-growth value is $13.45 per share. Against that, the business has grown revenue 8-9% a year (2024-2025), guides 6.9-7.7% for 2026, and the CARFAX half is growing 8%. Cash flow will grow slower than revenue in 2027 (margin -150bp, stand-up costs), but from 2028 on, with migration finished and the buyback shrinking the share count, 6% per-share cash growth is a reasonable base. So at 9% the market is pricing something modestly too pessimistic; at 8%, which suits an investment-grade, 81%-subscription data business, it is pricing almost no growth at all. The catch is that the stock already re-rated: it traded at $17.15 on 2026-09-30 and $24.04 on 2026-11-16 (yfinance), up 40% in seven weeks around the Q3 print and the buyback, so a good part of the forced-seller discount has already closed. Takeaway: the implied path (about 4% growth at 9%) is somewhat too pessimistic, but not by enough to call it a clear mispricing.

## 8. The thesis in one sentence, and why the market is not seeing it

Thesis: CARFAX is a 49%-margin used-car data toll growing 8% a year, sold with a slower B2B unit at 11.7x EBITDA against 13-20x for data peers, and a $350-450M buyback through 2027 (about 5-6% of shares at today's price) will compound per-share value even if the multiple never moves. What the market may not be weighting is how much of the drag is temporary: about $100M of one-time stand-up costs, a reversed sales bundle, and B2B margins hit by first-time standalone allocation all fall away or recover by 2028. But I cannot claim the market is blind: 12 analysts cover it, Eagle Capital (8.18%, 13G 2026-11-16), Gates Capital and Madison Avenue Partners bought into the spin (yfinance top holders at 2026-09-30), and the stock already rose 40% from the 2026-09-30 low. The visible discount to peers is explained by real things: leverage the peers mostly do not carry at this level, a 2.8x gap between adjusted and GAAP net income, a 12-year cash tax drag, and a B2B half that is barely growing its profit. Takeaway: the case is a slow compounder at a fair-to-modest price, not something the market is missing.

## 9. The industry picture

Automotive data is consolidating into a few owners of hard-to-rebuild records: CARFAX (service history, accidents, titles), Experian AutoCheck (auction and title data), Cox Automotive (Kelley Blue Book, Autotrader, Manheim auctions, vAuto; private), J.D. Power (private) and the marketplaces (CarGurus, Cars.com, Carvana) that sit on top. The direction of travel has three parts. First, used-car retail is moving online, which raises the value of trusted third-party information and the report-on-every-listing habit (Form 10 business section). Second, AI agents are a new channel and a new threat: management says CARFAX was "the most cited automotive site in LLMs among our competitive set" in Q3 and that LLM traffic grew double digits quarter over quarter but "remains small" (Q3 call); the risk is that an agent summarises history from other sources and the dealer stops paying for the badge. CARFAX's defence is that the raw service records are proprietary and licensed only to "reputable agents" on its terms. Third, data suppliers are consolidating and raising fees (Form 10 risk factors), which is the main pressure on CARFAX's margin. In B2B, automaker planning budgets are under pressure from tariffs and weak international demand ("new vehicle demand is holding up in the U.S. but remains soft internationally", CEO), lengthening sales cycles. Winners are the owners of exclusive raw data with consumer brands (CARFAX); losers are generic analytics and forecasting sellers whose output AI tools can approximate. MBGL owns one of each. Takeaway: CARFAX sits on the winning side of the shift, B2B on the contested side.
