## How money flows
A freight railroad owns the track, the yards and the locomotives, and sells space on its network to shippers: grain farmers and elevators, chemical plants, automakers, coal mines and utilities, and retailers moving containers (intermodal) from ports and warehouses. Revenue is volume (carloads or containers) times revenue per unit. Revenue per unit moves with three things: contract pricing, the mix of cargo (a coal car pays about $2,500 and an intermodal box about $1,600 at Union Pacific), and a fuel surcharge that is a pass-through of diesel cost. Costs are mostly fixed and slow to change: labour (about a third of an industry-wide cost index), depreciation of track and engines, and fuel (about 14% of the index). Because the network is a fixed toll road, extra volume on the same track drops through to profit, which is why operating ratio (operating cost as a percent of revenue, lower is better) falls when volume rises. Railroads do not pay for the roadbed through a public budget the way trucks use highways: they spend their own cash on track (UP plans $3.5B of capital spending in 2026), then return the rest to shareholders. Around the railroads sit suppliers who are paid by the railroads' capex: railcar builders (Trinity, Greenbrier), railcar lessors (GATX, Trinity), locomotive and signalling makers (Wabtec, Caterpillar's Progress Rail), track products (L.B. Foster) and intermodal marketers who buy rail space and sell door-to-door service (J.B. Hunt, Hub Group). This quarter the money story is fuel: diesel cost jumped, surcharges passed most of it through in dollars, but the pass-through inflates revenue and pushes the operating ratio up (UP says fuel cost it 140 basis points of operating ratio).
